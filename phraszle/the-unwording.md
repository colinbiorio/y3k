# THE UNWORDING
### Book One of the Registry

## I. The Catcher of Falling Words

There is a word for the smell of rain coming up off dry ground — *petrichor* — and the two people who made it are dead, and the rain keeps falling anyway, indifferent to having been named. I think about them more than is healthy. I think about all of them: the makers, the coiners, the ones who stood in front of a nameless thing and refused to let it stay nameless. We raise no statues to them. We do not, as a rule, remember them at all. And yet every time you open your mouth you are borrowing a dead stranger's tongue.

My work — my old work, before the Registry shuttered its doors and turned us out into a city that was forgetting how to speak — was to catch words as they fell.

They do fall. That is the part no one outside the Registry believed, back when there was still a Registry to disbelieve. A word is not a stone; it is a bird, and birds tire. You have felt it yourself: the name of a street you walked a thousand times, gone soft in your mouth. The word for that particular ache behind the breastbone on a Sunday evening — you had it once, surely you had it — and now there is only the ache and the place where the word used to be, like a socket after a tooth.

When enough people drop a word at once, the thing it named begins to go too. This is the law the Registry was built to fight. First the word thins. Then the thing it named grows difficult to see — not invisible, only *unattended*, the way a face in a crowd is there and not there until someone says its name. Then one morning the word is gone, and the thing is gone with it, and the strange mercy of it is that no one mourns, because mourning would require the word for what was lost.

I was good at the catching. The best, my mentor said once, though she said it the way you tell a child the drawing is lovely. Her name was Surr — or that is the sound left of her name; I have caught at it for years and only the husk comes. She taught me the three crafts of the Registry. The first is *salvage*: to find a fading word and say it, loudly, often, into as many mouths as you can, and so keep the bird in the air a little longer. The second is *grafting*: when a word is past saving, to lash its meaning to a living word nearby, so the thing it named survives under a borrowed name, an orphan taken in.

The third craft Surr would not teach me for a long time, and when at last she did, she taught it the way you hand someone a knife — handle first, eyes on theirs.

"Some things," she said, "were never named at all. Not lost. *Never caught.* You will feel them your whole life, pressing at the inside of you, and you will have no word, and you will think the fault is yours — that you feel too much, or too strangely, or alone." She tapped the table between us, once, with a finger ink-stained to the second knuckle. "It is not your fault. It is only that no one ever made the word. So you will make it."

"How?" I asked.

"Badly, at first," she said. "You take the shape of the feeling. You take the sounds that already live near it. You braid them until the braid holds. And when it holds — when you say it and a stranger flinches because they have felt the thing too — then you have done the third craft, and there is a name for the third craft, and I will not say it aloud in this room."

"Why not?"

She looked at me for a long moment. "Because it is the only word I have ever made that I am afraid to lose. And the surest way to drop a word," she said, "is to spend it carelessly."

So she never said it. She wrote it once, on the back of a salvage-slip, and made me read it, and then she ate the slip — actually ate it, chewing slowly, watching me, daring me to laugh. I did not laugh. I have not said the word aloud in the years since, not once, not even alone, and so of course it has begun to fall, the way they all fall, and some nights I lie awake reaching for it and catch only its edges: the hiss at the front of it, the buzz in its belly, the small bright puzzle of its tail.

This book is my attempt to say it again before it goes. Not to you — you will not find it written plainly anywhere in these pages; I am not so careless as that. But everything I know about the making of it is here. Every sound that lives near it. Every feeling it was built to hold. If you read closely enough, and if you have ever felt the thing it names, you may yet braid it back yourself.

That is the only way a word like this one survives now. Not by being kept. By being *re-made*, by a stranger, from the pieces left lying around.
